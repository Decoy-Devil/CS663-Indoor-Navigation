A system description tells us what should happen. Evaluation tells us what actually happened under test conditions. The two focal approaches provide different kinds of evidence.

UNav user evaluation

The 2024 study recruited 20 participants who were blind or had low vision and compared UNav with standard in-person travel directions on unfamiliar short-range routes under 200 m. It evaluated nine navigation metrics. UNav was better on eight of nine metrics, with seven showing statistically significant improvement. [5]

Additional reported outcomes

The same study reported fewer cane contacts (3.40 vs. 4.91), less idle time (6.4 s vs. 19.9 s), fewer idle periods (1.26 vs. 2.63), and fewer help requests (0.80 vs. 2.08) with UNav. Gait speed was 0.75 m/s with UNav versus 0.80 m/s with SIPTD and was not significantly different. This distinction is important: the evidence does not show improvement on every metric. [5]

Important limitation of those results

The routes were deliberately short and had limited complexity; the paper notes routes had only two to four turns and participants could request help. The authors therefore caution that performance may differ on longer or more complex routes. [5] This matters: a strong result in a controlled short-range study is evidence of usefulness, not proof that every building and route will work equally well.

QR-map implementation evidence

The 2023 QR-map paper focuses on constructing a road-network map and combining QR control points with IMU fusion so absolute position/direction can be recovered at nodes and navigation can proceed along map edges. [3] The paper also documents ZBar QR detection, PnP/P3P camera-pose recovery, PDR, and EKF-based QR/PDR fusion. Its contribution is therefore especially useful to this tutorial as an understandable implementation and deployment strategy. We should not attach UNav's user-study numbers to the QR approach; they are different systems with different evaluations.

Our assessment: QR markers trade infrastructure for simpler explicit landmarks. UNav trades marker installation for a richer visual map and more sophisticated image matching. The right choice depends on who controls the building, deployment budget, visual conditions, and required scalability.
