[1] Vision-based indoor navigation review

D. Khan, Z. Cheng, H. Uchiyama, S. Ali, M. Asshad, and K. Kiyokawa, “Recent advances in vision-based indoor navigation: A systematic literature review,” Computers & Graphics, vol. 104, pp. 24–45, 2022. DOI: 10.1016/j.cag.2022.03.005.

Synopsis: Systematic review of vision-based indoor-navigation research. It organizes methods, evaluation measures, challenges, and future directions and provides the conceptual background for localization, tracking, planning, and visual-navigation limitations used in this tutorial.

Reliability: High Peer-reviewed systematic literature review in an established journal; useful for broad synthesis, though individual implementation claims should still be checked against primary studies.

Publisher page

[2] User needs and indoor-navigation technologies

D. Plikynas, A. Žvironas, A. Budrionis, and M. Gudauskis, “Indoor Navigation Systems for Visually Impaired Persons: Mapping the Features of Existing Technologies to User Needs,” Sensors, vol. 20, no. 3, art. 636, 2020. DOI: 10.3390/s20030636.

Synopsis: Reviews indoor navigation technologies for visually impaired users and relates technical features to user needs. It distinguishes camera-based, non-camera-based, and hybrid approaches and discusses sensing, feedback, hardware, and deployment considerations.

Reliability: High Peer-reviewed open-access review with a documented literature search and a survey component involving blind experts. It is older than the two focal recent papers, so it is used mainly for background and taxonomy.

[3] QR-code map + IMU approach

R. K. Liu and D. J. Zhang, “Indoor Positioning and Navigation Based on QR Code Map,” International Archives of the Photogrammetry, Remote Sensing and Spatial Information Sciences, vol. XLVIII-1/W2-2023, pp. 665–670, 2023. DOI: 10.5194/isprs-archives-XLVIII-1-W2-2023-665-2023.

Synopsis: Proposes QR codes as indoor road-network nodes/control points and combines camera observations with IMU fusion. Defines node and edge records, recovers absolute pose from the four surveyed QR corners (ZBar + P3P), propagates position with Weinberg-model PDR, fuses both with an EKF, and plans routes with a Dijkstra-style search. Experiments report 0.08–0.25 m position and 1–4° heading error at 1–2 m from a code (decoding failed at 3 m) and show graphically that QR-aided fusion limits PDR drift on a 31-step route. Figures 1, 2, 4 and 5 are reproduced in this tutorial under CC BY 4.0.

Reliability: High for the described method Published in the ISPRS Archives with a DOI and clear method description. It is a short six-page conference paper with small experiments, no user study, and no summary error statistic for the fused trajectory. We therefore use it to explain architecture and engineering-level accuracy, not real-world efficacy.

Full text and PDF

[4] UNav technical system

A. Yang et al., “UNav: An Infrastructure-Independent Vision-Based Navigation System for People with Blindness and Low Vision,” Sensors, vol. 22, no. 22, art. 8894, 2022. DOI: 10.3390/s22228894.

Synopsis: Describes UNav's technical pipeline: query images, visual place recognition, reference imagery, location estimation, PnP-based direction estimation, topometric mapping, and Dijkstra shortest-path planning. The evaluation at an NYU Langone ambulatory care center (4,258 360° frames, 17 test points) reported average location error below 1 m, about 2° direction error, and 2–3 s per localization. Figures 1, 6 and 9 are reproduced in this tutorial under the article's CC BY 4.0 license.

Reliability: High Peer-reviewed open-access primary system paper with implementation and evaluation details. It is the appropriate source for explaining how UNav works.

Full text

[5] UNav user evaluation

A. Yang et al., “Evaluating the efficacy of UNav: A computer vision-based navigation aid for persons with blindness or low vision,” Assistive Technology, published online Aug. 13, 2024, DOI: 10.1080/10400435.2024.2382113; vol. 37, no. 6, pp. 459–473.

Synopsis: Evaluates UNav against standard in-person travel directions with 20 blind/low-vision participants on unfamiliar routes shorter than 200 m. Uses a non-inferiority crossover design at Mahidol University, Thailand (24 routes of 50–200 m with 2–4 turns). Reports nine spatial, temporal and confidence metrics with Wilcoxon tests, plus subgroup and ordering effects, server load and a 3.62 s mean round-trip latency. The freely accessible PMC copy is an NIH author manuscript, so its figures are linked rather than reproduced here.

Reliability: High Peer-reviewed user study with defined methods and statistical testing. Results are strong evidence for the tested short-range conditions but should not be generalized to every building or route.
